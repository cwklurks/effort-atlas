What Chirag's note is about — in four pictures

No formulas. Just the story.

1The question: how long do responses actually run?

Every answer a model writes has a length. If you run models for real (cost, speed, memory) or study them (like this paper), you want to know the spread of lengths — for example, what share of responses are shorter than some line you draw, like 256 tokens. Here, with all five lengths visible, you can just count: 3 of 5 are left of the line. Easy. The whole note is about what happens when you can't see the full bars.

2The problem: the system chops long answers, so the data lies

So you can't just count lengths from the logs. Throw the chopped rows away and you've deleted exactly the long answers — everything looks short. Treat the chopped length as the real length — same problem. Both easy fixes shrink your answer, sometimes by double.

3Doctors solved this exact problem 70 years ago

You'd never throw away the patients who outlived the study — they're your best evidence of long survival. Medicine built a standard tool (Kaplan-Meier, 1958) that uses "at least this long" information properly. Chirag's note is one sentence: a chopped response is a patient who outlived the study, so use the same tool.

4His experiment: break data on purpose, where you know the truth

That's the note: the chop is the problem, the doctors' tool is the fix, and the rigged demo is the proof — checked against a truth he built in on purpose.

And the link to your half of the paper: his curve says how often a given cap chops responses. Your experiment measures what that chopping does to accuracy scores. Same wall, two kinds of damage — that's why it's one paper.
