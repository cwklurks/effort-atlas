The intersection — one wall, two kinds of damage

Why his note and your experiment are one paper.

1The same wall breaks two different things

A chopped response damages two datasets at once: the length column (it records a floor as if it were a length) and the score column (a never-written answer counts the same as a wrong one). His note handles the first. Your experiment handles the second.

2How the wall fakes "thinking more makes it dumber"

Turning effort up makes answers longer, and longer answers hit the wall more. At the small cap the high-effort column collects more chopped non-answers — each one a zero that says nothing about reasoning quality. That's how a wall manufactures an "overthinking" curve. Your 78-response bug was this exact machine running silently.

3The test: raise the wall and watch the gap

Same items, same grading — only effort and wall height change. Like giving the careful student more exam time: if the gap closes, the clock was doing it; if it stays, careful thinking really was hurting. Either outcome is a publishable answer, which is why the result gets reported whichever way it lands.

4Why the two halves need different tools — the seam of the paper

Repair versus rerun. One wall, two consequences, one paper — and the seam between the halves is exactly the sentence "an unwritten answer isn't hidden data, it's no data."
