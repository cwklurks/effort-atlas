Section 3 · idea 2 — interactive

Kaplan-Meier: chain the survival odds at each checkpoint

A checkpoint is any length where someone actually finished. Step through them. At each one, the survival fraction uses only the responses still visible there (the “risk set”). Capped rows count in the denominator until they drop out — they never count as finishers.

Section 3 · idea 3

The curve you get — and the two things it refuses to tell you

No median: the median is where the curve crosses 0.5, and this curve stalls at 0.547 forever. No (unrestricted) mean: the mean is the total area under the curve out to infinity, and the shaded region's area is unknowable — the longest response was censored, so the tail could be short or enormous. The fix in the outline: report a restricted mean, the area up to a horizon τ you choose in advance (“average tokens generated within the first τ”).

Section 3 meets Section 4

Three estimates of F(256), one assumption-free bracket

Both naive strategies land at the edge of, or outside, the assumption-free bracket. KM lands inside it — if capping doesn't selectively remove would-be-long responses. And the “treat the cap as the length” mistake gets promoted: it is exactly the bracket's upper bound, a legitimate worst-case number rather than an error.

Section 4

Why C = 512 − prompt is a rigged raffle, and why strata un-rig it

The cap depends on the prompt, so the responses removed early cluster in one kind of conversation. If long prompts also breed long replies, the rows leaving the raffle are exactly the ones holding winning tickets — that's informative censoring, and pooled KM is biased. But inside each panel the cap is one constant: nobody is selectively removed, the raffle is fair by construction. So estimate per stratum, then average the curves back together. The price: without extra assumptions you only learn about lengths below 512 − (longest prompt) — that's the outline's “identified range.”

Section 5 · idea 1

[k/n, (k+u)/n]: how much of the score is hostage to truncation

Illustrative numbers from the exploratory pilot's max-effort cell: n = 30, k = 21 correct, u = 9 responses cut off with no final answer. The width u/n is the share of the cell's score that truncation alone leaves undetermined. Two cautions the doc states: this is bookkeeping, not prediction (a rerun with more room is a new draw and can also change existing answers), and k and u must not overlap — which is exactly why the final-answer rule has to be fixed before the run.

Section 5 · idea 2

The 2×2 is a difference-in-differences: compare the slopes

small output cap (pilot: 83.3% → 70.0%) large output cap (illustrative outcome)

Each line is the effort effect at one cap. Subtracting the slopes (I = D_large − D_small) cancels everything that hits both caps equally — item difficulty, base ability, the grader — leaving only the part of the effort effect that depends on the cap. Same design economists use for policy changes. If starvation inflated the small-cap slope, I comes out positive and unanswered length-stops should fall at the big cap, more for high effort. The large-cap line here is hypothetical: measuring it is the whole experiment.

Why “evidence consistent with,” never “proves”: the cap is a request parameter the model sees before it starts — like telling a student the exam length before the exam. Raising it doesn't just remove the scissors; it may change the pacing. The unanswered-stop mechanism check narrows that story but can't fully close it. That one sentence is the most important caveat in the paper.

One-breath summaries — Section 3: turn cap hits into “at least this long” observations and estimate the length curve by chaining local finish-rates. Section 4: grade that estimator on a corpus where the truth is known, and fix the window's dependence on prompt length by stratifying.
